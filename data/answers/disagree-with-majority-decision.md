**Short answer:** I would first make sure the people who disagree feel heard, privately if needed, and understand *why* they object, because a minority view often hides a real constraint (cost, religion, family, accessibility). Then I would look for a way to adjust the plan so it works for them too, or make participation optional with no penalty. A majority vote decides the default; it should not make anyone feel excluded.

## What they are checking

This is a Googleyness question about **inclusion and respect**, not about winning a vote.

- Do you notice and care about the people on the losing side?
- Do you get curious about the reason before you judge?
- Can you balance "the team decided" with "everyone still belongs"?
- For non-work matters (a team outing, a celebration, a dinner venue), do you avoid pressuring people?

## Structure

It is a hypothetical, so answer with a **clear approach**, then back it with a short real example if you have one.

1. **Listen:** talk to the dissenters one to one; groups make people quiet.
2. **Understand the reason:** dietary, budget, timing (caregivers, commute), cultural or physical accessibility.
3. **Adapt:** find a variant that keeps most of the majority's choice and removes the barrier (a different time, a vegetarian option, a cheaper venue, a remote-friendly part).
4. **Make opting out safe:** no comments, no tracking who came.
5. **Close the loop:** tell them what changed, and next time ask for constraints *before* the vote.

## Template

> "Say the team voted for [activity, e.g. a weekend outing at a bar], and [two people] were unhappy.
>
> I'd first speak to them privately and ask what doesn't work. Often it's something concrete, like [example constraint]. Then I'd go back to the organiser with an adjustment, for example [alternative], which keeps the spirit of the majority choice. If no single option works for everyone, I'd make it clear that skipping is completely fine, and maybe rotate the type of event next time.
>
> Something similar happened at [company / context] when [brief real situation]. I [what you did], and [outcome].
>
> The lesson for me is that a vote is a way to decide, not a way to make people feel outvoted. Now I [habit, e.g. collect constraints before the poll]."

## Mistakes to avoid

- "Majority rules, they should go along." This fails the round.
- Treating the dissenters as difficult people.
- Overriding the majority entirely to please one person, with no discussion.
- Inventing a story. A clear hypothetical approach is fine if you have none.
