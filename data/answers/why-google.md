**Short answer:** Give two or three reasons that are specific to Google and connected to you: the kind of problems (very large-scale, reliable systems), the engineering culture (design docs, code review, learning from strong engineers), and a product or area you actually care about. Then link it to your background as a Java backend engineer and where you want to grow. For the follow-up on conflicts with other teams, show you align on shared goals and settle disagreements with data and clear owners.

## What they are checking

- **Genuine motivation:** not prestige or salary.
- **Research:** you know something specific about Google's work or the team.
- **Fit:** a link between Google and your skills and goals.
- **Collaboration across teams** (the follow-up): Google is large, and much work crosses team boundaries.

## Structure

1. **Reason 1, the work:** a product, an area (cloud, infrastructure, search, ads systems) or the scale. Be specific.
2. **Reason 2, the culture:** a practice you value, and why.
3. **Reason 3, you:** how your experience fits and what you want to learn.
4. Keep it under a minute.

Google's mission is to organise the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful. You can link to it, but don't just recite it.

## Template

> "Three reasons.
>
> First, the problems. I've spent about five years building Java/Spring Boot services at [scale], and I want to work on systems where [specific interest, e.g. reliability and latency at very large scale] is the core problem. [Product / team] interests me in particular because [reason, e.g. I use it daily / I read about how it handles X].
>
> Second, the engineering culture: [practice, e.g. strong design-doc and code-review habits]. I've seen what [example from your experience, e.g. writing design docs before coding] did for my own team, and I want to work where it is the norm.
>
> Third, growth. I want to [goal, e.g. get much deeper in distributed systems], and working alongside engineers who've built these systems is the fastest way to do that."

**Follow-up, conflicts with other teams:**

> "With other teams, conflicts are usually about priorities or interfaces. I start from the shared goal, write down the options and their costs, and find who owns the decision. For example, [real story: a dependency team that couldn't change their API in time; you agreed an interim contract / adapter and a date]. If it is still stuck, I escalate jointly, with both leads, not around the other team."

## Mistakes to avoid

- "Google is the best company" or "the perks".
- Generic reasons that fit any big tech company.
- Naming a product you can't discuss further.
- Badmouthing your current employer.
