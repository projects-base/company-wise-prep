## Where we are

S7 gave us Spring Boot: one application that is easy to build and run. S7B showed what happened
when companies wired many applications together through a central bus: contracts were good, the
central "smart pipe" became a bottleneck. This lesson is the next swing: split the *one* big
application into small services that teams own end to end, and keep the pipes dumb.

## The problem

ShopKart is now a single Spring Boot app (a **monolith**: one codebase, deployed as one unit) with
50 engineers working in it. The symptoms:

- A one-line fix to the catalog waits for the weekly release, because everything ships together.
- The checkout team's change breaks the search team's tests, and both teams lose a day.
- Black Friday needs ten times more checkout capacity, but you can only scale the whole app.
- Upgrading a library means everyone upgrades at once.

None of these is about code quality. They are about **many teams sharing one deployable unit**.

## The idea: microservices

A **microservice** is a small application that does one business job (for example, Payments),
owns its own data, is deployed on its own, and talks to other services only over the network
through a published API.

```text
           ┌──────────── API gateway ────────────┐
           │                 │                    │
       Catalog            Orders              Payments
       (team A)          (team B)             (team C)
          │                  │                    │
       [Cat DB]           [Ord DB]             [Pay DB]
```

Compare with S7B: no central bus with business rules. The logic sits inside each service ("smart
endpoints"); the network just carries requests and events ("dumb pipes"). Docker (2013) made it
practical to package each service with its runtime, and Kubernetes (2014) to run hundreds of them.
S9 covers those.

## Principles (the why behind the patterns)

Learn these first; every pattern below follows from one of them.

1. **Fit the architecture to team size and scale.** A common path is monolith → modular monolith
   (S10) → services, moving only when the pain is real.
2. **Conway's law.** Melvin Conway (1968): a system's design tends to copy the communication
   structure of the organisation that builds it. So design service boundaries and team boundaries
   together.
3. **The network is not your friend.** The *fallacies of distributed computing*, collected at Sun in
   the 1990s, are eight false assumptions: the network is reliable; latency is zero; bandwidth is
   infinite; the network is secure; topology doesn't change; there is one administrator; transport
   cost is zero; the network is homogeneous. A method call never failed halfway. A network call can.
4. **Design for failure.** Assume every dependency will be slow or down at some point.
5. **High cohesion, loose coupling.** Things that change together live together; services know as
   little about each other as possible.
6. **Database per service.** Only Payments touches the payments tables. Others ask Payments.
7. **Twelve-factor apps.** A checklist published by Heroku engineers around 2011: config in
   environment variables, stateless processes, logs as streams, and so on.
8. **You build it, you run it.** Werner Vogels (Amazon CTO) used the phrase in a 2006 ACM Queue
   interview: the team that writes a service also operates it in production.
9. **Automate everything.** With 30 services, manual deploys and manual checks do not scale.

## The patterns, one problem at a time

Each row of this table is a family of problems. We take them in order, problem first.

| Problem | Patterns |
|---|---|
| Split | Business capability, DDD bounded context, strangler fig, anti-corruption layer |
| Talk | REST/gRPC/events, API gateway, BFF, service discovery |
| Survive | Timeout, retry+backoff+jitter, circuit breaker, bulkhead, fallback |
| Consistency | Saga, outbox, idempotent consumer, CQRS, event sourcing |
| Operate | Central config, health checks, tracing, correlation ids, sidecar/mesh |
| Change | Consumer-driven contracts, versioning, blue-green/canary, feature flags |

### Split: where do the lines go?

*Problem:* cut by technical layer ("the database service") and every feature touches every service.

- **Business capability**: one service per thing the business does (Catalog, Orders, Payments).
- **Bounded context** (from Eric Evans' *Domain-Driven Design*, 2003): a boundary inside which a
  word has one meaning. "Product" in Catalog (photos, description) is not "Product" in Warehouse
  (weight, shelf). Each context gets its own model, and often its own service.
- **Strangler fig** (named by Martin Fowler, 2004): don't rewrite. Put a router in front of the
  monolith and move one route at a time to a new service until the old code can be deleted.
- **Anti-corruption layer**: a translation layer at the edge of the new service so the monolith's
  messy model doesn't leak in.

### Talk: how do services call each other?

*Problem:* inside one process a call is free and reliable. Across the network, you must choose.

- **REST** (JSON over HTTP) for simple request/response; **gRPC** (binary Protocol Buffers over
  HTTP/2) for fast, typed internal calls; **events** (a message saying "OrderPlaced" on a broker)
  when the sender should not wait or care who listens.
- **API gateway**: one entry point for clients that handles routing, authentication and rate
  limiting. Keep business logic out of it, or you have rebuilt the ESB.
- **BFF (backend for frontend)**: a small API per client type (mobile, web) that shapes data for it.
- **Service discovery**: instances come and go, so a registry (or Kubernetes DNS) tells callers
  where a service lives right now.

### Survive: when a dependency is slow or down

*Problem:* Payments takes 5 seconds instead of 50 ms. Every Orders thread that calls it waits.
Soon all of Orders' threads are waiting, and Orders is down too, though nothing is wrong with it.
That is a **cascading failure**. You will reproduce it in the lab.

- **Timeout**: never wait forever. Give up after a bound you chose.
- **Retry with backoff and jitter**: retry transient failures, waiting longer each time
  (backoff) with randomness (jitter) so thousands of clients don't retry in lockstep. Only retry
  operations that are safe to repeat.
- **Circuit breaker**: after many failures, stop calling for a while and fail fast; then let a few
  trial calls through. Netflix's Hystrix library popularised this in Java; Hystrix went into
  maintenance mode in 2018 and Resilience4j is the usual choice now.
- **Bulkhead**: cap how many threads or connections one dependency may use, so it can't drain
  the whole pool. (Named after the walls that stop one flooded ship compartment sinking the ship.)
- **Fallback**: a degraded answer instead of an error: "payment pending", cached prices.

### Consistency: no more one big transaction

*Problem:* placing an order now writes to the Orders DB *and* the Payments DB. There is no
`@Transactional` across two databases owned by two teams.

- **Saga** (from Garcia-Molina and Salem's 1987 paper *Sagas*): a sequence of local transactions,
  each with a **compensating action** that undoes it if a later step fails.

```text
Create order (PENDING) → Reserve stock → Charge card → Mark order CONFIRMED
                              ↑ fails? ← Release stock ← Cancel order
```

  In **choreography**, each service reacts to the previous one's event. In **orchestration**, one
  coordinator tells each step what to do. Choreography has fewer moving parts; orchestration is
  easier to follow once there are more than a few steps.

- **Transactional outbox**: *problem:* you save the order and then publish "OrderPlaced"; the app
  crashes between the two, and the event is lost. *Fix:* in the same local DB transaction, insert
  the event into an `outbox` table. A separate relay reads the table and publishes. Now the order
  and its event are saved together or not at all.
- **Idempotency**: an operation is *idempotent* if doing it twice has the same effect as once.
  Networks redeliver, and clients retry. The client sends an **idempotency key** with "charge
  card"; the server stores the key with the result and returns the stored result on a repeat.
  Consumers of events do the same with event IDs (**idempotent consumer**).
- **CQRS** (command query responsibility segregation): separate write model and read model, so a
  read view can join data from several services' events.
- **Event sourcing**: store the list of events ("ItemAdded", "OrderPaid") as the source of truth
  and derive current state from them. Powerful, and a big commitment; use it only where history is
  the product (ledgers, audit).

### Operate: running 30 services instead of one

*Problem:* "checkout is slow." Which of the eight services involved is slow?

- **Central configuration** so you don't redeploy to change a setting.
- **Health checks** (liveness: "am I alive?", readiness: "can I take traffic?").
- **Correlation ID**: one ID created at the edge and passed on every call and log line, so you can
  grep one request across services.
- **Distributed tracing**: each hop records a timed *span*; together they show the request's path
  and where time went. (S9 covers OpenTelemetry.)
- **Sidecar / service mesh**: a helper process next to each service that handles retries, TLS and
  metrics for it, so each language doesn't reimplement them.

### Change: shipping without breaking each other

*Problem:* Team B changes an API field. Team A finds out in production.

- **Consumer-driven contract tests**: each consumer writes down what it relies on; the provider's
  build runs those checks (Pact and Spring Cloud Contract do this).
- **Versioning**: add fields, never silently remove or rename; run v1 and v2 side by side.
- **Blue-green**: run the new version beside the old, switch traffic at once, switch back if bad.
- **Canary**: send a small share of traffic to the new version first and watch the metrics.
- **Feature flags**: deploy code switched off; turn it on separately; remove old flags.

Two incidents worth reading in full: **Knight Capital (2012)**, where a deployment that missed one
server and a reused flag reactivated old code, losing over $400 million in under an hour (see the
SEC's 2013 order); and **CrowdStrike (July 2024)**, where a faulty content update crashed Windows
machines worldwide (see CrowdStrike's own post-incident review). Both are arguments for staged
rollout and for deleting dead code.

## When NOT to use microservices

- **Small team, one product.** If one team owns everything, the main benefit (team independence)
  doesn't exist, and you still pay the network costs.
- **Unclear domain.** Boundaries drawn too early are expensive to move. Learn the domain in a
  modular monolith first.
- **No operational maturity.** Without automated deploys, monitoring and tracing, 20 services are
  20 times the pain.
- **Strong consistency everywhere.** If most operations must be atomic across most data, you'll
  fight sagas all day.
- **"Because Netflix does it."** Netflix had Netflix's problems.

## Industry cases

Read the originals; these summaries only point you to them.

- **Amazon**: in the early 2000s moved to services behind APIs owned by small "two-pizza" teams.
  See the 2006 ACM Queue interview with Werner Vogels.
- **Netflix**: their 2016 post *Completing the Netflix Cloud Migration* says a major database
  corruption in August 2008 stopped DVD shipping for three days, which pushed them to AWS and
  services. They published Chaos Monkey (2011), which kills instances on purpose to prove the
  system survives, and Hystrix.
- **Uber**: grew to thousands of microservices, then described grouping them into domains in
  *Introducing Domain-Oriented Microservice Architecture* (Uber engineering blog, 2020).

## Lab (~10 min)

**Break it: a cascading failure, then the fix.** Java 21, no dependencies. Ten threads stand in for
Tomcat's worker pool.

```java
import java.util.concurrent.*;

public class Cascade {
    static final ExecutorService requestThreads = Executors.newFixedThreadPool(10);
    static final ExecutorService paymentsClient = Executors.newCachedThreadPool();
    static final Semaphore paymentsBulkhead = new Semaphore(3);

    static String slowPayments() throws InterruptedException {
        Thread.sleep(5_000);                      // Payments is having a bad day
        return "paid";
    }

    static String placeOrderNaive() throws InterruptedException {
        return slowPayments();                    // waits as long as Payments wants
    }

    static String placeOrderProtected() {
        if (!paymentsBulkhead.tryAcquire()) return "payment-pending (bulkhead full)";
        try {
            Future<String> call = paymentsClient.submit(Cascade::slowPayments);
            try {
                return call.get(1, TimeUnit.SECONDS);          // timeout
            } catch (TimeoutException e) {
                call.cancel(true);
                return "payment-pending (timeout)";            // fallback
            } catch (InterruptedException | ExecutionException e) {
                return "payment-failed";
            }
        } finally {
            paymentsBulkhead.release();
        }
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        boolean protectedMode = args.length > 0 && args[0].equals("protected");
        long start = System.nanoTime();
        for (int i = 0; i < 20; i++) {
            requestThreads.submit(() -> protectedMode ? placeOrderProtected() : placeOrderNaive());
        }
        Future<String> catalog = requestThreads.submit(() -> "catalog page");
        System.out.printf("%s served after %d ms%n",
                catalog.get(), (System.nanoTime() - start) / 1_000_000);
        requestThreads.shutdownNow();
        paymentsClient.shutdownNow();
    }
}
```

1. `java Cascade.java`: the catalog page, which never calls Payments, takes about 10 seconds. All
   ten threads were stuck waiting on Payments. That is the cascade.
2. `java Cascade.java protected`: the catalog is served almost at once. The bulkhead let only three
   threads wait on Payments, and the timeout freed them after a second.
3. In a real service you would use Resilience4j's `TimeLimiter`, `Bulkhead` and `CircuitBreaker`
   rather than hand-rolling these.

**Build (design, on paper):** the strangler fig. Put a gateway in front of ShopKart: `/payments/**`
goes to a new Payments service with its own database; everything else still goes to the monolith.
Write down how the monolith now asks for a payment, and what happens if Payments is down.

**Debate:** "15 engineers, one product: microservices?" Write a half-page decision record arguing
yes, then one arguing no. Which assumptions decide it?

## Gotchas

- **Distributed monolith.** Services that must deploy together, or share one database, give you
  all the costs and none of the independence. Test: can you deploy one without the others?
- **Retries without idempotency.** Retrying "charge card" without an idempotency key can charge
  twice. Make it idempotent first, then retry.
- **Retries without backoff.** Immediate retries from every client multiply load on a struggling
  service. Use backoff, jitter and a retry budget.
- **Publishing an event after commit, without an outbox.** A crash between the DB write and the
  publish loses the event. Use the outbox.
- **No timeouts.** Many HTTP clients default to very long or infinite timeouts. Set them on every
  remote call.
- **Splitting by layer.** A "database service" or "validation service" couples everything. Split
  by business capability.

Typical follow-ups:

- *"How do you keep data consistent across Orders and Payments?"* Strong answer: no distributed
  transaction; a saga with compensations, an outbox so events aren't lost, idempotent consumers
  because delivery is at least once, and a reconciliation job for what still slips through.
- *"Should we move to microservices?"* Strong answer starts with team count, deploy pain and
  domain clarity, not with technology, and offers the modular monolith as a middle step.

## Explain it in 2 minutes

**Prompt:** "What are microservices, what do they cost, and how do you keep them reliable?"

1. Small services per business capability, each owning its data, deployed independently.
2. The benefit is team independence and independent scaling, matched to Conway's law.
3. The cost: the network (fallacies), no cross-service transactions, heavy operations.
4. Survive: timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breaker, bulkhead, fallback.
5. Consistency: saga with compensation, outbox, idempotency keys.
6. When not to: small team, unclear domain, weak automation.

## Check yourself

1. What does "database per service" forbid, and why?
2. Payments is slow; why does Orders go down too, and which two patterns stop it?
3. What problem does the transactional outbox solve?
4. Saga choreography vs orchestration: one advantage of each.
5. Give two signs you have built a distributed monolith.

### Answers

1. Other services reading or writing Payments' tables directly. It would couple them to its
   internal schema, so it could no longer change or deploy alone.
2. Orders' threads all block waiting on Payments until none are left. Timeouts free threads;
   bulkheads cap how many can be stuck. (A circuit breaker then fails fast.)
3. Losing (or inventing) an event when the app crashes between the DB commit and the publish.
   The event is written in the same local transaction and published later.
4. Choreography: no central coordinator, simple for a few steps. Orchestration: the whole flow is
   visible in one place, easier to change and debug for longer flows.
5. Services must be released together; services share a database; one change needs edits in
   several services every time.
