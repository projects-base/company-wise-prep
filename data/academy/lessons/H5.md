## Where we are

The rest of the Academy teaches you to build things. INTV-TIPS-3 gave you habits for debugging them. This
lesson covers what changes at senior level: you are judged less on the code you write alone, and more
on the decisions you make, the risks you find early, and how much better the people around you get.
These skills work in any company and any domain.

## The problem

Two engineers both know Spring, Kafka and Kubernetes. One gets a vague request and builds exactly what
was said, weeks later, and it is not what was needed. The other asks three questions, writes one page,
spots a risk in a data migration, splits the work so that two juniors can help, and ships a smaller
version in a week.

The second engineer is senior. In interviews, this shows up as "Tell me about a technical decision you
made", and strong answers come from people who have practised these skills deliberately. They are
learnable, like any other skill.

## Turn ambiguity into a problem statement

A senior engineer's first job is often working out the actual problem. "Make checkout faster" could
mean many things. Ask:

- **What outcome do we want?** "Fewer abandoned checkouts" is a goal. "Add a cache" is a guess at a solution.
- **For whom, and how much?** "p95 under 1 s for mobile users" can be measured.
- **Why now?** The reason tells you how much effort is justified.
- **What is out of scope?** Saying this early prevents the arguments later.
- **What happens if we do nothing?** Sometimes that is the right answer.

Write the answer down in two or three sentences, and confirm it with whoever asked. Ten minutes here
regularly saves weeks.

## Design docs

A **design doc** is a short written proposal, shared *before* building. Its value is less in the document
than in the thinking and the review it forces. Changing a paragraph is much cheaper than changing a
deployed system.

Write one when the work is hard to reverse, touches other teams, or will take more than a few days. A
small bug fix does not need one. A good shape:

```text
Title, author, date, status
Context       What exists today, and why this matters now
Goals         What success means, with numbers
Non-goals     What this will deliberately not do
Proposal      The design: components, data, APIs, a diagram
Alternatives  What else you considered, and why you rejected it
Risks         What could go wrong, and how you would notice
Rollout       How it ships, how it rolls back, how data migrates
Open questions What you need others to decide
```

Two sections separate strong docs from weak ones. **Non-goals** show that you have thought about scope.
**Alternatives** show that you chose rather than defaulted. A doc with one option and no downsides is
a sales pitch, and reviewers will stop trusting it.

Keep it short enough to get read. Put the recommendation at the top, and ask specific reviewers for
specific input: "Priya, can the database handle this write pattern?" gets a far better reply than
"please review".

## ADRs: decisions that outlive you

Six months later someone asks why you chose Kafka over SQS. Nobody remembers. An **Architecture Decision
Record (ADR)** is a short file, kept in the repository, that records one decision and why it was made:

```markdown
# ADR 012: Use the transactional outbox for order events

Status: Accepted, 6 Nov 2026

## Context
Orders must publish events, and a direct publish after commit can lose them.

## Decision
Write events to an outbox table in the same transaction, and relay them to Kafka.

## Consequences
+ No lost events, and no distributed transaction.
- Consumers must handle duplicates, and there is a relay to operate.
```

ADRs are never edited to change history. If the decision changes, write a new one that supersedes the
old. **Consequences** matters most: it shows what you accepted knowingly, so a later reader can tell
whether the reasons still hold.

## Talking about trade-offs

Almost every senior answer has this shape: **"We chose X over Y, because of Z, and we accepted W."**

Compare "We used Redis because it is fast" with "We used Redis rather than a local cache, because we
have twelve instances and stale prices were unacceptable. We accepted a network hop and one more thing
to operate."

Two useful questions for any decision:

- **Is it reversible?** A reversible decision can be made quickly by one person: try it and change it
  later. A hard-to-reverse one, such as a data model or public API, deserves a slower process.
- **What is the cost of being wrong?** Spend your review effort where a mistake hurts most.

When you disagree, argue with evidence, using a measurement or a small prototype where you can. Once
the decision is made, **disagree and commit**: support it fully, and record your concern if it matters.

## Estimation and breaking down work

Estimates go wrong mainly because the work was not understood. So:

1. **Split into small pieces**, each deliverable and each ideally a day or two. Anything over a few days
   hides unknowns.
2. **Find the riskiest unknown and do it first**, using a short time-boxed **spike**: an experiment whose
   output is knowledge, not production code.
3. **Give a range and state your assumptions:** "Two to three weeks, assuming the vendor API works as
   documented." A single number sounds confident and is usually wrong.
4. **Add the work people forget:** tests, migration, rollout, monitoring, docs, review waits.
5. **Re-estimate out loud when you learn something.** Bad news delivered early is planning information.
   Delivered on the deadline, it is a failure.

Deliver in **vertical slices**: thin end-to-end pieces that work, not one layer at a time. A working slice
in week one exposes integration problems when they are cheap to fix.

## Technical debt

**Technical debt** is a shortcut that makes later work more expensive. Taking it can be a good decision
when it is deliberate, like a loan. The problem is debt nobody chose and nobody tracks.

To get time to fix it, translate it into business terms. "We need to refactor billing" loses to any
feature. "Each billing change takes two weeks rather than two days, and caused three incidents this
quarter" is a business case.

Prioritise debt by how often you touch that code, not by how ugly it is. Ugly code nobody changes costs
little. Fix debt as part of nearby feature work where you can, and leave the code a little better each
time.

## Code review

Review is one of the most effective ways to raise a team's quality, and it is where seniors have much of
their daily influence.

**As a reviewer:**

- Look at what matters most first: correctness, design, concurrency, security, failure paths, tests.
- Leave style to the formatter and the linter.
- Say what you mean, and why. Mark what blocks and what does not: "nit:" for optional points.
- Ask rather than accuse: "What happens if this call times out?" beats "This is wrong".
- Approve quickly. A review waiting two days costs more than most issues it finds.

**As an author:** keep changes small, explain *why* in the description, and review your own diff first.
Large PRs get skimmed. Small ones get read.

## Mentoring and raising the team

Your impact multiplies through others. A few things that work:

- **Ask before you answer.** "What have you tried? What do you think is happening?" builds problem-solving,
  where giving the fix only solves today.
- **Delegate outcomes, with support.** Give someone a stretch task with a clear goal and a check-in, and
  accept that they will do it differently from you.
- **Give feedback early, privately and specifically.** Describe what happened and its effect, rather
  than judging the person.
- **Make your knowledge available.** Pair on something hard, write it down, and run the walkthrough.
- **Give credit in public.** People notice who does.

If the team stops when you take a holiday, you have become a bottleneck, not a senior. Being
replaceable on any one system is a sign that you did this well.

## Leading an incident

When production breaks, someone has to coordinate. DIST-SYS-7 covered the diagnosis. Leading is separate:

1. **Declare it early,** and open one channel. Calling an incident that turns out small costs little.
2. **Assign roles:** an **incident commander** coordinates and does not debug, others investigate, and one
   person handles updates.
3. **Mitigate before you diagnose.** Roll back, turn off the flag, fail over. Stop the pain first, and
   find the root cause afterwards.
4. **Update on a schedule,** even with "no news, next update in 15 minutes". Silence makes people panic.
5. **Write down what happened as you go,** with timestamps.

Afterwards, hold a **blameless postmortem**. Blame teaches people to hide things, so ask how the system
let this happen rather than who did it. Cover the timeline, impact, causes, what went well, and a few
**specific action items with owners**. "Be more careful" is not an action item. "Add a staging check for
migrations, owned by Ravi, by Friday" is.

## Threat modelling

Security is cheapest during design. **Threat modelling** is a structured way of asking "how could this be
attacked?" before anyone builds it. WEB-API-4 taught the defences; this is how you decide where they go.
It takes four questions:

1. What are we building? Draw the data flows.
2. What can go wrong?
3. What will we do about it?
4. Did we do a good job?

Mark the **trust boundaries** on the diagram: every place where data crosses from less trusted to more
trusted, such as browser to API or service to third party. Most threats cluster there.

**STRIDE** is a checklist for question 2:

| Threat | Means | Example | Typical defence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S**poofing | Pretending to be someone | Stolen token | Strong auth, short-lived tokens |
| **T**ampering | Changing data | Editing a price in a request | Server-side checks, signatures |
| **R**epudiation | Denying an action | "I never refunded that" | Audit logs |
| **I**nformation disclosure | Exposing data | IDOR, secrets in logs | Authorisation, encryption |
| **D**enial of service | Making it unavailable | Flood of expensive queries | Rate limits, quotas |
| **E**levation of privilege | Gaining more rights | User reaches an admin API | Least privilege, checks on each call |

You will not fix everything. Rank each threat by likelihood and impact, fix the worst, and record the
rest as accepted risks, with an owner. Thirty minutes with a whiteboard during design finds a lot.

## Communicating with non-engineers

Product managers and leaders need decisions, risks and dates, not implementation detail:

- **Lead with the conclusion:** "We'll miss Friday by two days, because of the vendor's API. Options
  are A or B, and I recommend A."
- **Bring options, not only problems,** and include a recommendation.
- **Translate into impact:** users affected, money, risk and time.
- **Raise risks early,** when there is still time to act.
- **Say no with an alternative:** "Not by Friday, but we can ship the core flow by Friday and the
  reports next week."

The same style works in interviews, and it works for status updates, where three lines beat a long
message: what happened, what's next, and what you need.

## Lab (~10 min)

No code this time. Pick a real decision from your own work, or use "add a cache in front of the product
catalogue".

1. **Problem statement:** write the goal, the user, and a number, in three sentences.
2. **ADR:** write a short ADR for the decision, with at least one negative consequence.
3. **Alternatives:** name two options you rejected, and one honest reason for each.
4. **Threats:** draw the data flow, mark one trust boundary, and write one STRIDE threat for each letter.
5. **Say it:** give it out loud in the form "We chose X over Y, because of Z, and we accepted W", in under
   a minute.

If you can't name a downside in step 2, you have not finished thinking about the decision.

## Gotchas

- **Starting with the solution.** "We need Kafka" skips the problem. State the outcome first.
- **Design docs with one option.** They read as a sales pitch. Show real alternatives and downsides.
- **Single-number estimates.** Give a range with assumptions, and update it as you learn.
- **Nitpicking style in reviews.** It drowns out the real issues. Automate style.
- **Hero behaviour.** Fixing everything yourself feels good and creates a bottleneck. Teach instead.
- **Postmortems that name a person.** People stop reporting problems. Fix the system.

## Explain it in 2 minutes

"What makes someone a senior engineer rather than a mid-level one?"

- They turn vague requests into clear problems, and they ask why first.
- They make decisions with explicit trade-offs, and write them down.
- They find risks early: failure modes, security, rollout and migration.
- They break work into slices and estimate honestly.
- They make others better, through reviews and mentoring.
- They stay calm in incidents, and improve the system afterwards.

## Check yourself

1. What two sections most distinguish a strong design doc, and why?
2. What is the difference between a design doc and an ADR?
3. Why should the incident commander not be debugging?
4. What does each letter of STRIDE stand for?
5. How would you make the case for fixing technical debt?

### Answers

1. Non-goals show scope was thought through, and alternatives show a real choice with known downsides.
2. A design doc proposes a whole change before building. An ADR is a short permanent record of one
   decision, its reasons and its consequences.
3. Coordinating takes full attention: roles, updates and decisions. Someone deep in logs loses sight of
   the big picture.
4. Spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege.
5. Translate it into business impact: time lost per change, incidents caused, risk. Prioritise code
   that changes often.
