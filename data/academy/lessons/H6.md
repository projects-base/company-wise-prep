## Where we are

This lesson needs nothing before it. The other INTV-TIPS lessons prepare you for the technical rounds: coding
idioms, thinking aloud, system design.

This one covers:

- the rounds where nobody asks you to write code;
- the format of a whole interview loop, so you prepare the right thing for each round instead of practising
  LeetCode for a machine-coding round.

## The problem

You cleared the online test and two technical rounds. The last round is with the hiring manager.

Twenty minutes in she asks, "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a senior engineer." You talk for six minutes:

- the project background;
- the architecture;
- what the team decided;
- how "we" fixed it in the end.

She asks, "What did *you* do?" and you realise you never said. Then, "How did it turn out?" and you have no number,
only "it went well".

A week later the recruiter says the feedback was "strong technically, could not assess ownership".

Nothing you said was false. It was just not evidence.

This lesson turns your experience into short, reusable answers that are.

## What behavioural rounds check

**Predict:** an interviewer asks "Tell me about a conflict". Is she mainly interested in the conflict?

**Answer:** no. The question is a container.

She is scoring a few signals that predict how you will behave in the job, and the story is the evidence:

- **Ownership:** did you act without being told, and finish the job?
- **Judgement:** how did you decide under uncertainty, and what trade-off did you accept?
- **Collaboration:** how do you disagree, persuade, help and accept being wrong?
- **Communication:** can you explain something clearly to the person in front of you, in two minutes?
- **Learning:** what changed in how you work afterwards?
- **Level:** the scope of your stories (a task, a feature, a system, several teams) tells her which level you are
  working at.

So a weak answer is rarely a bad story. It is a story told without:

- the decisions;
- the "I";
- the result.

## STAR, and CARL as an alternative

**STAR** is the standard shape for an answer:

- **Situation:** one or two sentences of context. Only what the listener needs.
- **Task:** what *you* were responsible for, and why it was hard.
- **Action:** what *you* did, step by step, including the options you rejected. This is most of the answer.
- **Result:** what happened, with a number if you have one, and what you learned.

**CARL** (Context, Action, Result, Learning) is the same idea with Task folded into Context and an explicit
**Learning** step.

Use whichever you like; CARL helps when you tend to forget the "what I would do differently" part.

A rough time budget for a two-minute answer:

- Situation and Task about 20 seconds;
- Action about 70;
- Result and Learning about 30.

A **weak** answer to "Tell me about a time you improved a slow system":

> "We had a lot of performance issues in our project. The team looked into it and we found some database problems.
> We added caching and some indexes and things got much better. It was a good learning experience."

A **strong** answer to the same question (a generic example, not anyone's real story):

> "Our order-history API was timing out for large customers, and support tickets were growing every week
> (Situation). I owned the API, so I took the investigation (Task). I turned on SQL logging and found one query per
> order line, a classic N+1. I compared a join fetch against batch fetching, chose batch fetching because the join
> multiplied rows for big orders, and added an index on the foreign key. I also added a test that fails if the query
> count grows (Action). Slowest-5% latency dropped from about 4 seconds to under 400 ms and the tickets stopped.
> Now I check SQL counts in code review (Result, Learning)."

## A story bank: 6 to 8 stories, many questions

You cannot prepare an answer for every question, but you do not need to. Most behavioural questions ask for one of a
handful of themes.

A **story bank** is a short list of your own real stories, each written once in STAR form and tagged with the
questions it can answer.

A useful set of themes, each one a story from your own work (fill these in yourself; never borrow a story):

| Story theme | Questions it answers |
|---|---|
| The hardest technical problem you solved | most challenging project, a system failure, your strongest technology |
| A disagreement you resolved | conflict, disagreeing with a decision, a manager's unreasonable demand |
| A mistake or failure you owned | a failure, negative feedback, biggest disappointment |
| Delivering with pressure or little guidance | ambiguity, everything is P0, tech debt vs deadline |
| Helping or mentoring someone | an underperforming teammate, giving feedback, mentoring |
| Something you started without being asked | innovation, ownership, going beyond the role |
| Improving quality or process | code quality, a production incident's follow-up |
| Your current work, end to end | walk me through your work, why you are qualified |

Pick five questions from "In interviews" and say out loud which of your stories answers each.

Keep each story to bullet points, not a script.

A memorised script sounds memorised; bullets let you adapt it to the exact question.

## Round formats and how to prepare for each

**Predict:** you have a "machine coding" round next week. Should you practise hard LeetCode problems?

**Answer:** mostly not.

- Many companies run a loop of distinct rounds; names and order vary, and each tests something different.
- So always ask the recruiter for the exact loop.

A typical one:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  oa["Online assessment"] --> live["Live coding"]
  live --> mc["Machine coding / LLD"]
  mc --> hld["HLD"]
  hld --> hm["Hiring manager"]
  hm --> offer["Offer"]
```

| Round | What it tests | How to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Online assessment (OA) | DSA speed and correctness under a timer, sometimes MCQs on CS basics | Timed practice on common patterns; read constraints first; handle edge cases |
| Live coding (DSA) | Problem solving out loud, clean code, testing | Think aloud, brute force first, then optimise; dry-run with an example (INTV-TIPS-2) |
| Machine coding | A small working app in a fixed time, often around 90 minutes: running code, clean design, easy to extend | Practise full problems end to end in your IDE; a clean model and interfaces first; get it running, then extend |
| LLD | Classes, responsibilities, patterns, extensibility, sometimes concurrency | Requirements, then entities, then a class diagram, then core methods (LLD-DESIGN-5) |
| HLD | Architecture at scale: requirements, estimates, components, trade-offs | A fixed method and a few case studies (INTV-TIPS-4) |
| Hiring manager | Ownership, scope, how you work, motivation, team fit | Story bank, "walk me through your work", questions for them |
| Bar raiser | A cross-team interviewer who protects the hiring bar (Amazon calls it a Bar Raiser) | Deep behavioural follow-ups on your stories |

In a machine-coding round, a working, well-structured subset beats a half-finished complete design.

Interviewers often add a requirement at the end ("now add a second pricing rule"), and your design is judged on how
small that change is.

## Questions to ask the interviewer

At the end you will hear "Do you have any questions for me?" Good questions show you are choosing too, and they give
you real information.

Pick two or three that fit the interviewer:

- "What does a successful first six months look like in this role?"
- "What is the biggest technical challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "How does a feature go from idea to production here: who decides, how is it reviewed and released?"
- "How is on-call organised, and what was the last significant incident like?"
- (Hiring manager) "How do you give feedback, and how do people grow from this level to the next?"

Avoid questions the careers page answers, and leave compensation for the recruiter.

## Sensitive topics: leaving, gaps, notice and offers

These are short answers, not stories. Prepare them so they come out calm and positive.

- **"Why are you leaving?"** Talk about what you are moving *towards*, never complaints about your current employer.
  - Template: "I have learned a lot doing [current work]. I am looking for [specific thing this role offers:
    scale, domain, ownership], which is why this role interests me."
- **A gap in your CV.** Be brief and honest, and close with what keeps you current: "I took [time] for [reason].
  During that time I [course, project, certification], and I am ready to commit fully now."
- **Notice period.** State it plainly and say what is possible: "My notice period is [duration]. I can discuss an
  earlier release with my employer if the timing matters."
- **Offer discussion.**
  - Do your research on the role's market range before the call.
  - Let the company make the first offer where you can.
  - Ask for the full breakdown (fixed, variable, joining bonus, equity and its vesting), and ask for time to decide.
  - Negotiate on facts (a competing offer, the scope of the role), politely and in writing.

## Mistakes that cost offers

- **"We" all the way through.** The interviewer cannot tell what you did.
- **No result, or no number.** "It went well" is not a result. Use latency, errors, time saved, tickets, revenue,
  or at least a before and after.
- **A failure story with no failure.** "I work too hard" is not a weakness. Pick a real mistake and spend most of
  the time on what you changed.
- **Blaming others.** Even if a colleague was wrong, describe what *you* did about it.
- **Rambling.** Over three minutes without a pause. Stop after Result and let them ask follow-ups.
- **Inconsistency.** Telling the same story differently to two interviewers. They compare notes.
- **No questions at the end**, or no knowledge of what the company does.

## Lab (~10 min)

A design exercise, no code.

1. **Build.** Write the first three stories of your bank as bullet points under STAR (or CARL) headings, using
   only your own experience:
   - [Situation], [Task];
   - [Action 1, 2, 3 with one rejected option];
   - [Result with a number], [Learning].

   For each, list two bank questions from "In interviews" it can answer.
2. **Time it.** Say one story out loud with a timer.
   - Aim for under two and a half minutes.
   - If Situation alone takes over 40 seconds, cut it.
3. **Break it.** Rewrite one story replacing every "I" with "we" and drop the number from the Result.
   - Read it aloud and ask the follow-ups an interviewer would: "What did *you* do?", "How did you measure it?",
     "What would you do differently?"
   - Watch for the moment you cannot answer from the broken version: that missing detail is exactly what the strong
     version must say up front.
4. **Fix it.** Restore the "I" sentences, add the decision you made and the alternative you rejected, and record
   the final version in the story bank on the Progress page.

## Gotchas

- **Situation eats the answer.** Keep it to two sentences; the interviewer will ask if she needs more.
- **Preparing LeetCode for every round.** Ask for the loop, then prepare machine coding, LLD, HLD and behavioural
  separately.
- **Invented or borrowed stories** fall apart under the second follow-up. Use only what you did.

## Explain it in 2 minutes

*"How do you prepare for the behavioural and hiring-manager rounds?"*

- Rounds score signals (ownership, judgement, collaboration, communication, learning, level); stories are the evidence.
- STAR or CARL: most time on your own actions and decisions, and a measured result.
- A bank of 6 to 8 real stories as bullets, each tagged with the questions it answers.
- Prepare each round for what it tests; have short, positive answers for leaving, gaps and notice.

## Check yourself

1. What does CARL add compared with STAR?
2. Why does a story bank need only 6 to 8 stories?
3. How should you answer "Why are you leaving?"

### Answers

1. An explicit Learning step; Task is folded into Context.
2. Most questions are variations on a few themes, and one well-told story answers several.
3. Briefly and positively, about what you are moving towards, without criticising your employer.
