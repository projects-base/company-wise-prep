## Where we are

In S8 you split ShopKart into services that teams own, and learned the patterns that keep them
alive: timeouts, circuit breakers, sagas, the outbox, idempotency. Two things were left open: *how
do you run* dozens of services without a person babysitting each one, and *how do services talk*
without long chains of synchronous calls? This lesson covers the platforms and the event-driven
style that answered both, and what they cost.

## The problem

ShopKart has 25 services now. On a normal Tuesday:

- A server dies at 3 a.m. Someone has to notice, start the service elsewhere, and update the load
  balancer.
- Placing an order calls Orders → Inventory → Payments → Email → Loyalty, one after another. If
  Loyalty is slow, checkout is slow. If Email is down, checkout fails, even though the email could
  have gone out a minute later.
- "Checkout is slow" arrives as a ticket, and it takes a day to find which of the five services is
  at fault.

Three pains: **operating** many services, **coupling** through synchronous call chains, and
**seeing** what happens inside a request.

## Containers: one package per service

A **container** is a process that runs with its own isolated view of files, network and
resources, packaged together with everything it needs (JDK, jar, config). Docker (2013) made
building and running containers easy: a `Dockerfile` describes the image, and the same image runs on
a laptop and in production.

```dockerfile
FROM eclipse-temurin:21-jre
COPY target/orders.jar /app/orders.jar
ENTRYPOINT ["java", "-jar", "/app/orders.jar"]
```

The rule: **ship the runtime with the app**, so "works on my machine" becomes "works on every
machine that runs containers". The S7 fat jar fits this neatly: one jar, one image.

## Orchestration: Kubernetes

Containers solve packaging, not the 3 a.m. problem. A **container orchestrator** decides which
machine runs each container, restarts crashed ones, and routes traffic to healthy ones.

**Kubernetes** was announced by Google in 2014, building on lessons from Borg, Google's internal
cluster manager (described in the 2015 paper *Large-scale cluster management at Google with Borg*).
It is now run by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (CNCF, founded 2015).

The key idea is **desired state**: you declare "I want 3 copies of Orders, version 1.4", and
Kubernetes keeps working to make reality match.

```text
You declare:   replicas: 3, image: orders:1.4
Reality:       2 running (one machine died)
Kubernetes:    starts a third on another machine; stops sending traffic to the dead one
```

The readiness and liveness checks from S8 are how Kubernetes knows what "healthy" means. Rolling
updates, canaries and autoscaling are built on the same loop.

**Cloud native** is the umbrella name for this style: containerised services, run by an
orchestrator, configured declaratively, designed to be replaced rather than repaired.

## Serverless: no servers to manage

**Serverless** (functions as a service) goes one step further: you upload a function, and the
cloud runs it per event and bills per invocation. AWS Lambda launched in 2014; Azure Functions and
Google Cloud Functions followed. It suits spiky or rare work (image resizing, webhooks). The
costs: cold starts (the first call after idle is slow, a real issue for JVM start-up),
time limits, and bills that can exceed a plain server for steady, heavy load. S10 has a case.

## Events: tell, don't ask

Back to the call chain. Does Orders really need to *wait* for Email and Loyalty? No. It needs to
announce a fact: "order 7 was placed". Whoever cares can react.

An **event** is a record of something that happened, named in the past tense (`OrderPlaced`).
In **event-driven architecture**, services publish events to a broker and other services
subscribe; the publisher doesn't know who listens.

```text
Before:  Orders → Inventory → Payments → Email → Loyalty   (each waits for the next)

After:   Orders ──publishes OrderPlaced──► [ broker ]
                                             ├──► Inventory
                                             ├──► Email      (down? catches up later)
                                             └──► Loyalty
```

Email being down no longer breaks checkout. Adding a new listener (Analytics) needs no change to
Orders.

## Kafka: the log as a broker

**Apache Kafka** was built at LinkedIn and open-sourced in 2011. Jay Kreps' 2013 essay *The Log:
What every software engineer should know about real-time data's unifying abstraction* explains the
thinking; it's worth reading.

Kafka's core idea is the **log**: an append-only list of records. Messages are not deleted when
read; each reader keeps its own position.

- **Topic**: a named log, like `orders`.
- **Partition**: a topic is split into partitions so many machines can share the work. **Order is
  guaranteed only within one partition.**
- **Key**: the producer sends a key (like `orderId`); the same key always goes to the same partition,
  so all events for one order stay in order.
- **Offset**: a record's position in its partition. A consumer remembers the last offset it handled.
- **Consumer group**: several instances of one service that split the partitions between them.
- **Lag**: how far a consumer's offset is behind the newest record. Growing lag means it can't keep up.

```text
topic "orders"
  partition 0: [o-3 Placed][o-3 Paid][o-9 Placed] ...
  partition 1: [o-7 Placed][o-7 Cancelled] ...          ← all of order 7, in order
  partition 2: [o-4 Placed] ...
                        ▲ Inventory's offset    ▲ Email's offset (each reads at its own pace)
```

Because the log is kept, a new service can **replay** history from offset 0.

## Delivery guarantees

Networks and crashes mean a consumer may see a message twice or never. The three levels:

- **At most once**: never twice, may be lost.
- **At least once**: never lost, may be duplicated. The usual default.
- **Exactly once**: Kafka offers idempotent producers and transactions *within Kafka*, but side
  effects outside it (sending an email, writing to your DB) still need your own protection.

So the S8 rule comes back: **make consumers idempotent**, usually by recording processed event IDs.
Messages that keep failing go to a **dead-letter topic** for a human to inspect, instead of blocking
the partition forever.

## Seeing inside: tracing

With events, a single order touches six services across seconds or minutes. Logs alone won't tell
the story. **Distributed tracing** gives each request a trace ID; every service records **spans**
(timed steps) under that ID and passes the ID on, over HTTP headers or event headers. Google's
*Dapper* paper (2010) described the approach. **OpenTelemetry** (formed in 2019 by merging the
OpenTracing and OpenCensus projects) is today's vendor-neutral standard for traces, metrics and logs.
For Java, its agent can instrument Spring, JDBC, HTTP clients and Kafka clients without code changes.

## Service mesh

A **service mesh** puts a proxy (a *sidecar*) next to every service instance. The proxies handle
mutual TLS, retries, timeouts and metrics, configured centrally. Envoy (from Lyft), Istio and
Linkerd are well-known examples. It removes that code from every service, in every language, at the
price of another complex system to run and debug.

## The new pain

Cloud native solved "operate many services". It brought:

- **Eventual consistency**: after `OrderPlaced`, Inventory is updated *soon*, not *now*. UIs and
  people must cope with that.
- **Harder debugging**: no single stack trace; the cause may be a message from an hour ago.
- **Cost and complexity**: Kubernetes, Kafka, a mesh and tracing are each a system to learn, run
  and pay for. A small team can spend more time on the platform than the product.
- **Premature splits**: when services are easy to create, teams create too many. That sets up S10.
- **Blast radius**: shared platforms fail big. The **AWS S3 us-east-1 outage (February 2017)** began
  with a mistyped command during maintenance that removed more capacity than intended (AWS's own
  summary is public). Read it for "guard your tooling" and "limit blast radius".

## Industry cases

- **LinkedIn → Kafka (2011)**: read *The Log* (2013) for why a log unified their data pipelines.
- **Kubernetes (2014) and Borg**: read the Borg paper (EuroSys 2015) for the ideas Kubernetes kept.
- **Discord**: *How Discord Stores Trillions of Messages* (2023) describes moving from Cassandra to
  ScyllaDB and adding data services in front of the database. Notice that the fixes are about
  hot partitions and tail latency: data layout matters as much as architecture.

## Lab (~10 min)

**Break it: duplicates and ordering.** A three-partition log, in memory, Java 21.

```java
import java.util.*;

public class MiniLog {
    record Event(String eventId, String orderId, String type) {}

    static final int PARTITIONS = 3;
    static final List<List<Event>> log = new ArrayList<>();
    static int roundRobin = 0;

    static void publish(Event e, boolean useKey) {
        int p = useKey ? Math.floorMod(e.orderId().hashCode(), PARTITIONS)
                       : roundRobin++ % PARTITIONS;
        log.get(p).add(e);
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) {
        boolean useKey = args.length > 0 && args[0].equals("keyed");
        boolean idempotent = args.length > 1 && args[1].equals("idempotent");
        for (int i = 0; i < PARTITIONS; i++) log.add(new ArrayList<>());

        publish(new Event("e1", "order-7", "OrderPlaced"), useKey);
        publish(new Event("e1", "order-7", "OrderPlaced"), useKey);   // producer retried
        publish(new Event("e2", "order-7", "OrderCancelled"), useKey);

        int stock = 10;
        Set<String> processed = new HashSet<>();
        // Partitions are consumed independently; here the last one happens to be fastest.
        for (int p = PARTITIONS - 1; p >= 0; p--) {
            for (Event e : log.get(p)) {
                if (idempotent && !processed.add(e.eventId())) {
                    System.out.println("p" + p + " skip duplicate " + e.eventId());
                    continue;
                }
                switch (e.type()) {
                    case "OrderPlaced" -> stock--;
                    case "OrderCancelled" -> stock++;
                    default -> { }
                }
                System.out.println("p" + p + " " + e.type() + " -> stock " + stock);
            }
        }
        System.out.println("final stock = " + stock + " (correct answer: 10)");
    }
}
```

Run all three and compare:

1. `java MiniLog.java`: no key. The cancel is processed *before* the order exists (stock 11), and
   the duplicate takes stock to 9. Both bugs at once.
2. `java MiniLog.java keyed`: ordering is fixed (one partition), but the duplicate still leaves 9.
3. `java MiniLog.java keyed idempotent`: correct. In production, `processed` is a table written in
   the same transaction as the stock change.

**Then think:** what if the consumer for partition 1 is ten minutes behind? Which user-facing screen
shows wrong data, and what alert (on lag) would catch it?

Optional, later: repeat with a real Kafka broker in Docker and the OpenTelemetry Java agent, and
find the order's trace across Orders, Inventory and Email.

## Gotchas

- **Assuming global ordering.** Kafka orders within a partition only. Key by the entity whose
  events must stay in order.
- **Assuming exactly once end to end.** Your side effects still need idempotent consumers.
- **One poison message blocking a partition.** Retry a few times, then send it to a dead-letter
  topic and alert.
- **Not watching consumer lag.** A slow consumer looks healthy until users notice stale data. Alert
  on lag, not just on errors.
- **Events that are really commands.** "SendEmailNow" from Orders couples Orders to Email again.
  Publish facts (`OrderPlaced`); let the subscriber decide.
- **Adopting the whole platform at once.** Kubernetes, Kafka and a mesh on day one can swamp a small
  team. Add each when a real pain demands it.

Typical follow-ups:

- *"How do you guarantee per-user ordering?"* Strong answer: key by user ID so a user's events share
  a partition; one consumer per partition; note the cost (a hot user makes a hot partition).
- *"What happens if a consumer crashes mid-batch?"* Strong answer: it restarts from the last
  committed offset, so some messages are reprocessed; idempotency makes that safe.

## Explain it in 2 minutes

**Prompt:** "What does cloud native mean, and why use events instead of direct calls?"

1. Containers package service plus runtime; Kubernetes keeps declared state (restarts, scaling).
2. Synchronous chains couple availability and latency of every service in the chain.
3. Events announce facts; subscribers react independently; new subscribers need no publisher change.
4. Kafka: partitioned log, ordering per key, consumers track offsets, replay is possible.
5. At-least-once delivery means idempotent consumers and dead-letter topics.
6. Costs: eventual consistency, harder debugging, platform complexity; tracing helps.

## Check yourself

1. What does "desired state" mean in Kubernetes?
2. Why does Kafka need a message key to keep an order's events in order?
3. Name the three delivery guarantees and which one you usually get.
4. What is consumer lag, and why alert on it?
5. Give one case where a synchronous call is still the right choice.

### Answers

1. You declare what should be running; the orchestrator keeps acting until reality matches.
2. Ordering only holds within a partition; the key decides the partition, so the same key keeps
   events together and in order.
3. At most once, at least once, exactly once; at least once is the usual default.
4. How far the consumer is behind the newest message. Growing lag means stale data downstream,
   with no errors to warn you.
5. When the caller needs the answer to continue, such as checking a price or authorising a
   payment before confirming to the user.
